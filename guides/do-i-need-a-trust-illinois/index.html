Do I need a trust in Illinois?

Many Illinois families do well with a will, powers of attorney, and current beneficiary forms. A trust adds the most when there are minor or vulnerable beneficiaries, a blended family, real estate in more than one state, privacy or incapacity concerns, or an estate near the Illinois $4 million line. It also adds cost and upkeep, so the question is fit.

Start with the size of your estate

Size sets the baseline. The steps below are not to scale. Each person’s estate counts separately, and everything you own is counted, including life insurance and retirement accounts.

  1. Under $150,000
    Personal property in your name alone

    A small estate affidavit may let your family collect bank accounts, investments, and other personal property without formal probate. Registered vehicles are not counted toward the limit. The affidavit covers personal property only, never real estate. A house or land needs probate or another transfer method, such as joint title, a transfer on death deed, or a trust. 755 ILCS 5/25-1

  2. Up to $4 million
    Below the Illinois estate tax line

    A will, powers of attorney, and current beneficiary forms often do the job, especially with no minor children. Joint title, payable-on-death accounts, and beneficiary forms can carry much of the transfer. A trust is then a question of fit, not tax. 35 ILCS 405/2

  3. Over $4 million
    Illinois estate tax applies

    Once an estate passes this line, Illinois figures its tax on the whole taxable estate and a return is required. For married couples, credit shelter and marital trusts are where a trust begins to save tax. The Illinois Attorney General’s calculator shows the figure. 35 ILCS 405/2, 405/3

  4. Over $15 million
    Federal estate tax applies (2026)

    The federal exemption is $15 million per person for deaths in 2026. It is adjusted for inflation in later years, so the figure changes. Generation-skipping trusts, life insurance trusts, and lifetime gifts come into play. Married couples can combine exemptions with the right plan. 26 U.S.C. 2010; IRS

Three roads for your property

What a trust changes is the road your property takes. Anything you own still follows one of these three.

A trust only controls the middle road for property that has been moved into it. Everything else keeps taking the top or bottom road.

Situations where a trust tends to help

A trust can hold and manage the money until ages you choose. Without one, a court-supervised guardianship may be needed, and a young heir may receive the money outright at 18.

Leaving them money directly can cost them benefits. A special needs trust holds it for them instead, and it is usually its own trust, not a clause in a general one.

A trust can provide for a spouse while protecting what passes to your own children. Without one, a surviving spouse controls what is left.

Real estate titled in a trust can avoid a second probate in the other state.

A will becomes part of the public record when it is probated. A trust is usually a private document.

A named successor trustee can take over trust assets without a court. Powers of attorney are still needed for everything outside the trust.

A discretionary trust lets a trustee decide when and how much to pay out, and a spendthrift clause can limit what the beneficiary’s creditors can reach.

Credit shelter and marital trusts can reduce estate tax for couples, and a life insurance trust can provide cash so assets do not have to be sold.

A clear written plan with a named trustee can reduce confusion. It cannot prevent every disagreement, which is why the choice of trustee matters.

What a trust does not do

It does nothing until it is funded

A trust controls only what has been retitled into it. A bank account (personal property) or a house (real estate) still in your name alone can still need probate. A pour-over will catches what was missed, but it still goes through probate.

It does not save estate tax by itself

Assets in a revocable trust are still counted in your estate for tax. Trusts save tax only when they are built for it, such as a credit shelter and marital trust for a couple above the exemption levels.

It does not shield assets from creditors

During your life a revocable trust’s property is open to your creditors. After death, it can be reached for claims, costs, and statutory awards when the probate estate is not enough. 760 ILCS 3/505

It does not replace your other documents

You still need a will to catch stray property, a power of attorney for property, a power of attorney for health care, and a living will. Powers of attorney cover decisions the trust cannot.

Common questions

Does a trust avoid probate in Illinois?

Property titled in the name of the trust’s trustee generally passes without probate. Property left outside the trust, such as a bank account or a house still in your name alone, can still need probate, which is why a trust has to be funded. A pour-over will catches what was missed, but it still goes through probate.

Is a living trust the same as a will?

No. A will directs property in your name alone at death, becomes public in probate, and is where you can nominate a guardian for minor children. A revocable living trust holds property during your life and after death under terms you set, and is usually private. Most trust-based plans still include a will.

Do I need a trust if my estate is small?

Often not. With $150,000 or less in personal property, a small estate affidavit may avoid formal probate for that property. It does not cover real estate, which needs probate or another transfer method such as joint title, a transfer on death deed, or a trust. Joint ownership and beneficiary forms can carry much of the plan. A trust may still fit for other reasons, such as a child with a disability, young beneficiaries, or real estate in another state.

Does a revocable living trust save estate tax?

Not by itself. Its assets are still counted in your estate. Trusts save tax only when they are built for it, such as a credit shelter and marital trust arrangement for couples above the exemption levels. Illinois estate tax starts at $4 million, and the federal exemption is $15 million per person for deaths in 2026.

Can creditors reach a revocable trust?

Yes. During your life, a revocable trust’s property is open to your creditors. After death it can be reached for claims, costs, and statutory awards to the extent your probate estate is not enough. A revocable trust is not an asset protection tool.

Who should serve as trustee?

Any competent adult can serve, and a corporate trustee is an option for long or complex trusts. The choice deserves even more thought than choosing an executor, because a trustee may manage the trust for many years. Name a successor trustee in case the first one cannot serve.
