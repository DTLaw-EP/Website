What happens if you die without a will in Illinois?

If you die without a will in Illinois, state law decides who inherits: your spouse and children first, then parents and siblings, then more distant relatives. Unmarried partners and stepchildren are generally not on the list. A court picks who runs the estate, and your own wishes are not part of the decision.

Who inherits? Pick a family

Illinois decides by a fixed list, found in the Probate Act. Choose the picture closest to your family to see how it plays out. Gold outlines show who inherits, dashed boxes show who does not.

You Inherits Not an heir

Married, with children

Your spouse receives one-half of the estate. Your children, and the descendants of any child who has died, share the other half equally. This is true even if the children are not your spouse’s children. The picture shows two children, so each receives one-quarter. (755 ILCS 5/2-1(a))

Married, no children

When you leave a spouse and no descendants, your spouse receives the entire estate. Your parents and siblings receive nothing from assets that pass under these rules, even if they are living. (755 ILCS 5/2-1(c))

Children, no spouse

With no spouse, everything goes to your descendants. The law splits it “per stirpes,” which means a child who died before you is replaced by that child’s own children, who share the parent’s portion. Here, the living child takes one-half and the two grandchildren split the other half. (755 ILCS 5/2-1(b))

No spouse and no children

Your parents, brothers, and sisters share equally. If one parent has died, the surviving parent takes a double portion, and a deceased sibling’s children take that sibling’s share. With both parents living and two siblings, each of the four receives one-quarter. (755 ILCS 5/2-1(d))

Unmarried partner or stepchildren

The list is built from marriage, birth, and legal adoption. An unmarried partner and stepchildren are not on it, no matter how long the relationship or how close the family. Without a will, the estate passes to your closest relatives who are on the list. If no relatives can be found, it goes to the county and the State. (755 ILCS 5/2-1)

The order Illinois follows

Each step applies only if there is no one at the step above it. A spouse is the exception, because a spouse shares with children but takes everything when there are none.

  1. Spouse and children. One-half to the spouse, one-half to the children and their descendants.
  2. Children and their descendants. Everything, if there is no spouse.
  3. Spouse. Everything, if there are no children or other descendants.
  4. Parents, brothers, sisters. Equal shares; their children stand in for a deceased sibling.
  5. Grandparents and their descendants, split between the mother’s and father’s sides.
  6. Great-grandparents and their descendants, split the same way.
  7. Nearest other relatives. Equal shares among the closest in degree.
  8. The county and the State. Only if no relatives can be found.

Who is not on the list

  • Stepchildren. They are not treated as children unless legally adopted. Illinois courts recognize “equitable adoption” only in narrow cases with clear and convincing proof.
  • An unmarried partner. No matter how long you have lived together.
  • Friends, charities, and in-laws. A will or trust is how you name them.
  • Your wishes. Without a will the court follows the statute, not what you told your family.

Two lanes: what the default rules do and do not control

Even with no will, many assets never go through these rules. They pass according to the paperwork behind them.

Follows the default inheritance rules

Property in your name alone, with no named beneficiary.

  • Bank account in your name only
  • House or land titled only to youOften needs probate
  • Car, jewelry, furniture, and other personal items
  • Accounts that name “my estate” as beneficiary

Goes to the person on the paperwork

These pass whether or not there is a will.

  • Joint tenancy with right of survivorship
  • Payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts
  • Retirement accounts and life insurance with a named beneficiary
  • Assets already titled in a living trust

What happens in probate when there is no will

1Someone asks the court to open the estate

A petition is filed. With no will, there is no executor to name.

2The court appoints an administrator

This person gathers and protects the assets. It may not be who you would have chosen.

3Creditors are notified

Notice is published and mailed. The claim deadline is at least six months after first publication.

4Debts paid, then the rest is divided

The administrator distributes what is left by the statute, and the court closes the estate.

Six months is the usual minimum. Many estates take eight months to a year or more.

An estate with personal property of $150,000 or less (not counting registered vehicles) may be able to use a small estate affidavit instead of formal probate. That can skip the court process, but it does not change who receives the property. The same list applies. (755 ILCS 5/25-1)

Young heirs and minor children

If a child is an heir, a court-appointed guardian may be needed to manage the money, and the child may receive it outright at 18. A will lets you keep that money in a trust for longer and choose who manages it. A will is also where a parent can nominate a guardian for a minor child. A nomination that is signed and witnessed like a will is treated as valid on its face, and the court still decides based on the child’s best interests. Without a will, there is no nomination at all. (755 ILCS 5/11-5)

A will is only the first layer. A trust, powers of attorney, and up-to-date beneficiary designations cover what a will cannot, including what happens if you become unable to manage your own affairs.

Common questions

I am married with children. Who inherits if I have no will?

Under the Illinois Probate Act, your spouse receives one-half of your estate and your children (and the descendants of any child who has died) share the other half. This applies even if the children are not your spouse’s children.

If I am married with no children, does my spouse get everything?

Yes. If you leave a spouse and no descendants, Illinois law gives the entire estate to your spouse. Your parents and siblings receive nothing from the assets that pass under these rules, even if they are living.

Do my stepchildren or unmarried partner inherit if I have no will?

Generally no. The Illinois list is built from marriage, birth, and legal adoption. Stepchildren and unmarried partners are not on it. Courts recognize “equitable adoption” only in narrow cases and require clear and convincing proof, so it is not something to rely on.

Who runs my estate if I have no will?

A court appoints a personal representative, called an administrator when there is no will. With a will, you choose the executor yourself and can ask that no bond be required. The administrator gathers assets, notifies creditors, pays debts, and distributes what is left under the statute.

What if I have no living relatives?

If no spouse and no known relatives can be found, Illinois law sends the estate to the county and the State. Real estate goes to the county where it is located, and other property goes to the county or the State Treasurer as unclaimed property.

Do bank accounts with a beneficiary and jointly owned property follow these rules?

No. Assets that pass by contract or title, such as joint tenancy with survivorship, payable-on-death accounts, and retirement accounts or life insurance with a named beneficiary, go to the person on the paperwork. The default inheritance rules apply to what is left in your name alone.
