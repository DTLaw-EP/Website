The Life Cycle of Families

When life changes, your plan should too.

An estate plan is not a one-time purchase. It is a living record of who your family is today. These are the moments when families most often need a fresh look, and the documents that tend to need attention. Open any event to see what changes, and tick the ones that apply to you for a personal review list.

Stage 1

Starting out together

Two lives, and often two sets of paperwork, come together.

1Marriage or committing to a partnerSoon after

Documents you signed before may name people who are no longer your closest circle. If you are not married, your partner generally has no automatic right to inherit from you or to make medical decisions for you. If you are married, a spouse has rights under Illinois law that may or may not match what you intend.

Worth reviewing

  • Who receives your property under your will, and who is named executor
  • Beneficiaries on retirement accounts, life insurance, and bank accounts
  • Your health care and financial agents
  • How a home or joint accounts are titled
  • Whether a prenuptial or cohabitation agreement fits your situation
2Buying a homeAt closing

The way you take title decides who receives the home and whether it has to go through probate. It is much easier to choose on purpose at closing than to fix it later.

Worth reviewing

  • How title is held: individually, jointly, in a trust, or with a transfer on death instrument
  • What happens if one owner dies or becomes unable to manage the property
  • Whether life or disability insurance covers the mortgage
  • Whether your will or trust needs to be updated to reflect the new asset

Stage 2

Growing the family

Children and new relationships raise the stakes for every document.

3A new child or adoptionRight away

A child cannot manage an inheritance, and if both parents were gone, a court would decide who raises your child unless you have said otherwise. This is the single most common reason parents come in.

Worth reviewing

  • A guardian, and a backup, named for your child
  • Whether a trust should hold money for your child until an age you choose
  • Beneficiaries on life insurance and retirement accounts
  • Enough life insurance to replace income and cover your child’s needs
  • Who controls any education savings account
4New family members: a blended familyBefore or soon after

When a family combines, the default rules rarely fit. Stepchildren generally inherit nothing from a stepparent unless they are named. A surviving spouse also has rights that can compete with children from an earlier relationship. Without a plan, good intentions can turn into family conflict.

Worth reviewing

  • How to provide for a spouse for life while protecting what passes to your own children, often with a trust
  • Who is named guardian, executor, and trustee, and whether they are neutral enough
  • Beneficiaries on accounts that bypass your will
  • Life insurance used to balance what each part of the family receives
  • Which assets are shared and which stay separate
5Your child turns 18Before the 18th birthday

At 18, a young person becomes a legal adult. Parents lose automatic authority to speak with doctors, see medical records, or handle money, even if the child is away at school or has a disability.

Worth reviewing

  • A health care power of attorney and records release for your young adult
  • A financial power of attorney
  • For a child with a disability: whether guardianship, or a less restrictive alternative, is the right fit
  • Whether public benefits apply, and how an inheritance should be left to protect them

Stage 3

Building a livelihood

Work changes what you own, what you owe, and what your family relies on.

6Business growthAt each milestone

As a business grows, so does the share of your wealth, and your family’s income, that depends on it. Without a plan, a death or disability can freeze the business at the moment it is most vulnerable.

Worth reviewing

  • Who runs the business if you cannot, and who owns it afterward
  • Operating or shareholder agreements, and buy-sell terms with partners
  • Whether business interests should be held in a trust
  • Key-person and life insurance to fund a transition
  • A financial power of attorney that covers business decisions
7Change of careerWhen you start the new role

A new job or self-employment changes your benefits, retirement accounts, and risks, and it often creates new accounts that start with an empty beneficiary form.

Worth reviewing

  • Beneficiary forms on the new employer’s retirement plan and life insurance
  • What happens to old retirement accounts you leave behind
  • If you become self-employed: a business entity, insurance, and a plan for continuity
  • Whether your life and disability coverage still match your family’s needs
8Job lossAs soon as you can

When a job ends, employer-provided life and health coverage may end with it, just when a family is most exposed. You may not be able to afford a full plan right now, but the basics matter more, not less.

Worth reviewing

  • Whether group life or disability insurance stops, and how to replace it
  • Decisions about retirement accounts, and who is named as beneficiary on any new account
  • That health care and financial powers of attorney are in place, so someone can act if you are ill or stressed
  • Which documents can wait, and which cannot

Stage 4

When life changes suddenly

Illness, disability, and the end of a relationship can arrive without warning. Documents must be signed while you are able to sign them.

9Serious illnessAs soon as possible

After a serious diagnosis, the most valuable step is to put decision-makers and wishes in writing while you can. Documents have to be signed while you understand what you are signing, and that window can close.

Worth reviewing

  • A health care power of attorney and living will that reflect your wishes
  • A financial power of attorney so bills, taxes, and property are managed
  • Whether a trust would let someone step in without court involvement
  • Long-term care costs and how benefit rules, including look-back periods, could affect planning
  • Beneficiary forms, so they match your current wishes
10Disability, yours or a loved one’sAt diagnosis, and at every transition

Public benefits such as SSI and Medicaid have strict limits on money and property. A gift or inheritance left directly to a person with a disability can cost them those benefits. The right tools protect both the benefits and the person.

Worth reviewing

  • A supplemental (special needs) trust, so an inheritance adds to benefits instead of replacing them
  • Who will care for your loved one, and who will manage the money, after you
  • Guardianship, or a less restrictive alternative, for an adult who needs support
  • A letter of intent that tells future caregivers who your loved one is
  • Whether other relatives’ wills accidentally leave money outright to your loved one
11SeparationBefore making changes

A separation does not by itself cancel your documents. Your spouse may still be your agent, your beneficiary, and your executor. At the same time, changes made while a case is pending may be restricted, so this is a moment to coordinate carefully.

Worth reviewing

  • Who is currently named in your will, powers of attorney, and beneficiary forms
  • What your family law attorney or any court order allows you to change right now
  • Securing copies of documents and account information
  • A short-term plan for your children if something happens to you
12DivorceWhen the divorce is final

Illinois law cancels some gifts and roles for a former spouse automatically, but not everything. Accounts with a named beneficiary, such as retirement plans and some insurance, may still pay a former spouse unless you change them. A final decree is the moment to start fresh.

Worth reviewing

  • A new will or trust that reflects your life now
  • New health care and financial agents
  • Beneficiary forms on every account and policy
  • Guardian choices for your children, and provisions for their inheritance
  • Title to your home and accounts

Stage 5

Later chapters

The focus shifts to passing things on well and to being cared for.

13RetirementIn the year before and after

Retirement turns a lifetime of saving into a source of income and an inheritance. The rules for retirement accounts matter, because most non-spouse heirs must now empty an inherited account within ten years, with tax consequences.

Worth reviewing

  • Beneficiary designations on every retirement account, and whether a trust should be named
  • Powers of attorney, since you may rely on others more as you age
  • Long-term care planning and how you would pay for it
  • Downsizing, moving, and how any new property is titled
  • Charitable giving and legacy wishes
14Death of a loved oneWithin weeks, then within months

A death starts two tasks at once: settling the estate, and updating your own plan. If you are named as executor or trustee, there are deadlines, including filing the will with the court. If you are the survivor, the person you relied on may have been your agent, your guardian choice, or your beneficiary.

Worth reviewing

  • If you are an executor or trustee: what to file and by when, and what duties you now have
  • Replacing agents, executors, and guardians who can no longer serve
  • Beneficiary forms and the title to accounts and property
  • Where an inheritance you receive should go, especially if a family member has special needs
