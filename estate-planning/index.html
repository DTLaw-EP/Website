Estate planning

Estate planning for families as they really are

DT Law helps Naperville families put a plan in place: a will, financial and health care powers of attorney, a living will, and, when it fits, a revocable living trust. Each plan is written for the family you actually have, whether blended, unmarried, growing, or caring for a loved one with special needs.

What is happening

Without a plan, Illinois law decides who makes decisions for you if you cannot, who raises your minor children, and who receives your property. Those default rules rarely match a modern family. Stepchildren, unmarried partners, and loved ones who receive public benefits are common examples where the defaults fall short.

What can be done

  • A will names who receives your property, who serves as executor, and who would guard minor children.
  • Financial and health care powers of attorney name the people you trust to act for you, and a living will records your wishes for end-of-life care.
  • A revocable living trust can hold property privately, may help avoid probate for assets titled in it, and can manage money for young or vulnerable beneficiaries.
  • A review of beneficiary forms and how property is titled, so the pieces of your plan work together.
  • Guidance on a business succession plan, when you own a business.

Common questions

What documents make up a basic estate plan?

A basic plan usually includes a will, a financial power of attorney, a health care power of attorney, and a living will. The Foundation Plan also includes a transfer on death instrument for real property.

Do I need a will or a trust?

A will controls what passes through probate and names an executor and guardians for minor children. A revocable living trust can hold property privately and may help avoid probate for assets titled in it. Which fits depends on your family, your assets, and your goals, and we decide together at your consultation.

What if my family is blended or unmarried?

Default rules often fall short for stepchildren and unmarried partners. A plan can name them, protect a surviving spouse while preserving what passes to your children, and appoint the people you trust to make decisions.
